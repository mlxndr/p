## Where Are We?

* Last week, we looked at how clauses contain phrases, and how those phrases have a *function* within a clause (Subject, Predicator, Object, Complement, Adverbial)
* Recap:
  * Phrase structure is about the *internal* structure of phrases (eg inside a NP, what functions as modifier, headword or neither?)
  * Clause structure is about how phrases *combine* in larger structures, such as sentences (eg is the NP functioning as the Subject or the Object of a clause?)

--

## The Levels of Grammar | (The ‘Rank Scale’)

* **Sentence:** Made up of main *clause(s)* plus possible subordinate *clause(s)*
* **Clause:** Made up of *phrases* acting as Subject/Predicator/Object/Complement/Adverbial
* **Phrase:** Made up of *words* acting as headwords or modifiers in a Noun Phrase/Verb Phrase/Prepositional Phrase/Adverb Phrase/etc.
* **Word:** Noun/Verb/Adjective/Preposition, etc.

--

## Okay, what is a clause?

* The key characteristic of a clause is that it *must* contain a VP functioning as Predicator
* We can think of clauses as being built up around Predicators; a clause is essentially a Predicator with its associated (optional) SOCA elements
  * Often, but by no means always, the basic structure is SPOC with As cropping up anywhere in between
* And the main thing about clauses is that they are the elements which make up a *sentence*

---

## How do clauses make sentences?

* Firstly, **simple sentences** are those which consist of one clause (ie, one Predicator)

@card ex
* He awoke in the night.
* I love grammar.
* Why do you hate me?
* She really should go to the shops today.
* The wolves will never stop hunting me.
@end

--

## How do clauses make sentences?

* But not all sentences are simple; some contain more than one clause
* These are **compound** sentences and **complex** sentences
  * Compound sentences involve coordinating clauses together
  * Complex sentences involve clauses which are embedded inside other clauses

--

## Complex vs Compound

* Compound sentences use the **coordinating conjunctions** *and, or, but*, or *nor*
* Complex sentences have **main clauses** and **subordinate clauses**
  * Main clauses could be simple sentences, or parts of compound sentences
  * Subordinate clauses are embedded in a main clause, and can’t stand on their own
  * They use subordinating conjunctions, such as *after, although, as, because, before, if, how, however, like, once, since, than, that, though, till, unless, until, when, where, while,* etc.
  * Subordinate clauses do the job of a clause element – that is, a subordinate clause can be a Subject, Object, Complement or Adverbial within a main clause – and some can work as modifiers inside a phrase

--

## Complex vs Compound

@card ex
* He **awoke** in the night and **discovered** he **loved** grammar.
* I **loved** grammar when I first **read** about it.
* I **love** grammar but I don’t **love** my wife.
* What you<strong>’re saying</strong> about grammar **is** awesome.
* You **can kick** my dog, you **can insult** my family, you **can steal** my wine, but you **can’t insult** grammar in my house.
  * Note the commas: coordinating conjunctions can be left out altogether (Leech calls this *unlinked coordination*). The commas here are doing the same job as *and*.
* After we **learn** grammar, we **can take on** the world.
* It **would be** a shame if sex and rock music **distracted** us from grammar.
@end

--

## Decision Process

1. Count the Predicators
   * One Predicator? One clause: simple sentence
   * More than one Predicator? Look for *and, or, but, nor* (and for commas doing the same job as *and, or, but, nor*)
2. Check what the conjunction actually joins – words and phrases can be coordinated too
3. Test each clause on its own
   * If every one could stand alone: compound sentence
   * If **any one** cannot: it is subordinate and the sentence is complex

--

## Sentences and Clauses

* Simple sentence
  * One Predicator, one clause (must by definition be a main clause)
  * [Bill is happy. He loves grammar. Luckily, he easily found similar friends.]{ex}
* Compound sentence
  * Two+ Predicators, two+ main clauses, coordinating cj(s) (*and, or, but, nor*)
  * [Bill is a grammarian and loves his job. He does grammar all day, but also has other interests like phonetics.]{ex}
* Complex sentence
  * Two+ Predicators, one+ main clause(s), one+ subordinate clause(s) which act as SOCA, subordinating cj(s) (*because, although, unless, if, when, whilst, whether,* etc)
  * If a sentence contains both coordination and a subordinate clause, it is complex. A subordinate clause anywhere makes the whole sentence complex.
  * [Because grammar makes him happy, Bill is content. He is never happier than he is when parsing sentences.]{ex}

---

## Recognising Subordinate Clauses

* What if there is no subordinating conjunction?
* Look for non-finite verbs (see UGiST) such as *to go, going, having gone,* etc:
  * [**Going to** the shops, I realised I had lost my puppy.]{ex}
  * [**To be** honest, I thought he was right next to me.]{ex}
  * [**Having lost** my puppy in Waitrose, I cried like a baby.]{ex}
  * [I was delighted **to see** my puppy again.]{ex}
* Look for a wh- element (*who, whom, whose, what, which, when, where, how*) at the start of the clause:
  * [**What you’re saying about grammar** is awesome.]{ex}
  * [I know **which book you mean.**]{ex}
  * [The student **who sat at the front** asked a good question.]{ex}

--

## Recognising Subordinate Clauses

* Subordinate clauses can be replaced by phrases which carry out the same clause function:

@card ex
* I told her about my lost puppy.\
  I told her the story.\
  I told her something.
* Going to the shops, I realised I had lost my puppy.\
  That day, I realised I had lost my puppy.
* To be honest, I thought he was right next to me.\
  Genuinely, I thought he was right next to me.
* Having lost my puppy in Waitrose, I cried like a baby.\
  All day I cried like a baby.
* I was delighted to see my puppy again.\
  I was delighted by it.
@end

## Labelling Clauses

* Phrases get surrounded by round brackets (), clauses by square brackets [], sentences by squiggly brackets {}

  * There are names for the subtypes of subordinate clause - see *UGiST*
* Coordinating conjunctions sit outside of clauses and phrases
* Subordinating conjunctions sit outside of phrases but inside the subordinate clause
* Sentence = Se

## Simple and complex grammatical styles

* Different grammatical styles (simple and complex):
  * have different processing demands
  * have a different degree of formality
  * are associated with speech (simple) and writing (complex)
  * signal clause relationships differently
