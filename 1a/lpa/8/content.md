## Prescriptive and descriptive

* **Prescriptive**: statements about how language should be used
* **Descriptive**: statements about how language is used

@card ex
* The plural of *octopus* {==should==} be *octopuses*.
* Most people in this room said *octopi*.
@end

## The American Heritage Usage Panel

* The *American Heritage Dictionary* (1969) answered *Webster’s Third* with a Usage Panel of 105 writers, editors and scholars
* 11 of the 105 were women; 6 were under 50
* Panellists voted on ballots of example sentences; the editors turned the results into usage notes
* Nearly 200 members by the end; dissolved on 1 February 2018

* Leaked to ITV News in July 2019, when he became Leader of the House
* Banned: *very*, *due to*, *equal*, *lot*, *got*, *hopefully*, *I am pleased to learn* and others
* Imperial measurements; a double space after a full stop; *Esq.* for men without a title

## GOV.UK: words to avoid

“Plain English is {==mandatory==} for all of GOV.UK so {==avoid==} using these words:”

* *deliver*: “pizzas, post and services are delivered - not abstract concepts like improvements”
* *key*: “(unless it unlocks something)”
* *slim down*: “(unless talking about one’s waistline)”
* *drive out*: “(unless it’s cattle)”
* *going/moving forward*: “(it’s unlikely we are giving travel directions)”

## Hansard’s house style

* Correct “this” to “the”, and remove other deictics
* Correct “this chamber” to “this Committee” or “this House”
* Omit otiose repetition (“very very”)
* Remove otiose hedges such as “I believe” and “I think”
* Government is always plural

## Swearing and slurs

* Discriminatory language is more problematic for audiences than swearing
* Terms about race, nationality and ethnicity are among the least acceptable
* Intent is one of the strongest influences on what is acceptable
* The watershed is still seen as an important safeguard

## Better and worse ways

* **Subtractive**: a new variety replaces the old one; **additive**: a new variety is added to the speaker’s repertoire (Lambert 1974)
* **Face**: the public self-image a speaker wants to keep; a correction threatens it, more so in public (Brown & Levinson 1987)
* **Linguistic insecurity**: speakers who believe their own speech is wrong (Labov 1966)

@pause

* Glasgow pupils’ judgements of Scots sentences as “incorrect” fell after a discussion of Scots
* Cushing (2020): school bans discourage pupils from speaking at all
