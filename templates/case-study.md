## Case Study: Standard Structure

### Case Study 1: The Helsinki Corpus Project

**Background**
* Launched 1984, University of Helsinki
* Goal: Create diachronic corpus of English (Old to Early Modern)
* Team of 15+ researchers across multiple institutions

**Context & Challenge**
* Pre-digital age: limited computational tools
* Manuscript sources fragmentary and difficult to access
* No established standards for historical corpus compilation

**Approach**
* Stratified sampling across periods and text types
* Manual transcription and encoding
* Careful documentation of sources and editorial decisions

**Outcomes**
* 1.5 million words, 400+ text samples
* Became foundational resource for historical linguistics
* Enabled quantitative studies of language change
* Inspired similar projects worldwide (ARCHER, COHA, etc.)

**Lessons Learned**
* Importance of explicit sampling methodology
* Value of comprehensive metadata
* Need for collaborative infrastructure

## Case Study: Longitudinal Development

### The Evolution of Wikipedia's Editorial Norms (2001-2024)

**Phase 1: Founding Era (2001-2004)**
* Minimal formal rules ("ignore all rules")
* Rapid growth, enthusiasm-driven
* Emerging conflicts over content quality

**Phase 2: Systematization (2005-2010)**
* Core policies formalized (NPOV, Verifiability, No Original Research)
* Administrator roles established
* Increased bureaucratization

**Phase 3: Mature Governance (2011-2018)**
* Complex dispute resolution procedures
* WikiProjects for domain expertise
* Tension between inclusionists/deletionists

**Phase 4: Contemporary Challenges (2019-2024)**
* Addressing systemic bias
* Gender gap among editors
* Vandalism and misinformation management
* AI-generated content policies emerging

**Analytical Insight:** Trajectory from charismatic to rational-legal authority (Weber); demonstrates how digital communities develop institutional structures over time

## Case Study: Failure Analysis

### The Demise of Google+ (2011-2019)

**Ambition**
* Facebook competitor with 90+ million users at peak
* Integration across Google ecosystem
* Emphasis on "Circles" for selective sharing

**What Went Wrong**

*Strategic Missteps:*
* Forced integration with YouTube, Gmail (user resentment)
* Unclear value proposition vs. established platforms
* Late entry into mature market

*Design Flaws:*
* Confusing privacy controls
* Ghost town problem (low engagement despite registrations)
* Real-name policy alienated potential users

*Organizational Issues:*
* Internal politics and competing priorities
* Insufficient commitment of resources
* Frequent leadership changes

**Aftermath:** Shut down April 2019; valuable lessons for platform design

**Lessons for Researchers:** Even well-resourced projects fail without clear user need, network effects critical for social platforms

## Case Study: Best Practice Example

### Success Story: Duolingo's Freemium Model

**Context:** Language learning app launched 2011

**Innovative Approach:**
* Free, ad-supported basic version
* Premium subscription ($7/month) removes ads, adds features
* Gamification and behavioral psychology principles
* Crowdsourced translations (early monetization strategy)

**Key Success Factors:**

1. **Accessibility:** Low barrier to entry, mobile-first design
2. **Retention:** Streak system, push notifications, daily goals
3. **Efficacy:** Research-backed methodology, adaptive learning
4. **Business Model:** 5-8% conversion to premium; sustainable economics
5. **Continuous Improvement:** A/B testing, user data analytics

**Results:**
* 500+ million registered users (2024)
* 40+ languages offered
* IPO 2021, valued at $6.5 billion
* Research shows effectiveness for basic proficiency gains

**Replicable Principles:** Behavioral design, freemium economics, data-driven iteration

## Case Study: Comparative Deep Dive

### Two Models of Open Access Publishing

<div class="container">
<div class="col">

#### PLOS ONE Model

**Launch:** 2006

**Approach:**
* Author-pays APC ($1,900)
* Peer review for soundness, not significance
* Massive scale (>200,000 articles)
* CC-BY license

**Strengths:**
* Rapid publication
* Reduces publication bias
* Truly open access

**Challenges:**
* Quality perception issues
* High costs for unfunded researchers
* Editorial sustainability

</div>
<div class="col">

#### arXiv Model

**Launch:** 1991

**Approach:**
* Free for authors and readers
* Preprint server, no peer review
* Physics, math, CS focus
* Institutional/grant funding

**Strengths:**
* Immediate dissemination
* Zero cost to users
* Community moderation

**Challenges:**
* No formal peer review
* Quality variability
* Not accepted by some institutions for tenure

</div>
</div>

**Implications:** No one-size-fits-all solution; disciplinary culture matters; ongoing evolution of scholarly communication

## Case Study: Theoretical Application

### Applying Bourdieu's Habitus to Elite University Admissions

**Theoretical Framework**
* **Habitus:** Embodied dispositions and cultural competencies
* **Cultural Capital:** Knowledge, skills, education valued by dominant class
* **Field:** Social arena with specific rules and stakes

**Case Context:** Analysis of admission essays from 200 applicants to selective university

**Analysis:**

*Working-class applicants emphasized:*
* Individual achievement against odds
* Family sacrifice narratives
* Explicit career instrumentalism

*Upper-middle-class applicants emphasized:*
* Intellectual curiosity "for its own sake"
* Cultural experiences (travel, arts)
* Leadership in extracurriculars

**Findings:** Admissions criteria favor upper-middle-class habitus (25% acceptance vs. 11%); apparently "meritocratic" process reproduces class advantage

**Theoretical Insight:** Demonstrates how Bourdieu's concepts illuminate hidden mechanisms of social reproduction in seemingly objective contexts
